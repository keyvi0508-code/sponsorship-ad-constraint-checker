## 1. Problem statement

I focus on the work of brand and creator-partnership reviewers who check sponsored short-form video scripts against creator-facing brand rules. A keyword search can find literal phrases, but it can miss meaning expressed through slang, metaphor, or context. An AI reviewer may interpret context, but it can also over-escalate acceptable content or miss a violation. I built and evaluated a human-in-the-loop prototype that compares a deterministic keyword-search baseline with an AI-assisted reviewer on the same small, controlled set of scripts.

I designed the prototype to help a reviewer find evidence and decide what needs attention. My local browser workbench accepts a script draft and presents keyword and AI findings side by side; my batch evaluator runs the same reviewer over frozen cases. I keep AI calls behind an explicit cost confirmation and do not use the tool to make final approval decisions.

**Dataset note:** The course's primary evaluation is the original balanced 30-case synthetic set. I also created a separate, blind-reviewed 30-case extension. I report the primary result first and the extension as supplementary development evidence; I do not combine their scores or present either as real-world performance.

## 2. User, scope, and source

I designed for a brand partnerships/content-policy reviewer at a luxury beauty company who checks a creator's proposed English caption and spoken script before publication. This is my hypothesis about the user, not a persona validated through interviews. I assume the reviewer already has the brand rules but needs to locate relevant wording and decide what needs checking. My project is an independent academic prototype and is not affiliated with or approved by CHANEL. I use CHANEL's publicly available [Social Media Guidelines](https://www.chanel.com/us/makeup/social-media-guidelines/), accessed on 29 September 2026.

I compared my concept with [Aspire's creator-content approval feature](https://www.aspire.io/content-creation): its vendor documentation describes content approval, reviewer comments, change requests, and approval before creators post ([review workflow](https://help.aspireiq.com/en/articles/10370759-reviewing-content-in-group-review-as-a-content-approver)). This is a broader campaign workflow, not evidence that Aspire lacks rule analysis. My prototype is narrower: I check submitted text against a small rule map and return quoted evidence and a proposed disposition. I do not manage creator campaigns, review full video/audio, or communicate revisions to creators. I use a measured Ctrl+F-style keyword baseline as my non-AI comparator. I have not measured how many scripts a real team handles or its current review time, so I make no claim about industry-wide time savings.

I check disclosure wording and sponsor identity, disclosure placement represented in structured text fields, and whether criticism of a competitor could be presented as impartial. I can route unsupported factual or first-hand product claims to human review. My prototype cannot inspect finished video or audio, verify on-screen visibility or spoken audibility, access private campaign briefs, or confirm unpublished selling points. The official guide may change after my recorded access date.

## 3. Design and business/technical trade-offs

I separate deterministic evidence checks from policy judgement. Level 1 checks the structured input and gathers candidate evidence. Level 2 applies my source-grounded rubric and returns `PASS`, `FLAG`, or `HUMAN_REVIEW`, with a rule ID, quoted evidence, and rationale. I constrain the AI output to a structured schema and route ambiguous cases to a person.

I use the keyword baseline because it is cheap, reproducible, and easy to explain, while recognizing that it depends on patterns chosen in advance and is weak on paraphrase and context. My AI reviewer can interpret more varied wording, but introduces API cost, latency, nondeterminism, and the risk of unsupported interpretation. I keep human review in the workflow to reduce the risk of treating uncertainty as automatic approval, while recognizing that it consumes reviewer time. I therefore use AI for decision support and escalation, not as an autonomous gate.

My build-versus-buy choice is deliberately hybrid: I own the command-line interface, Python orchestration, rule mapping, synthetic cases, and evaluation code; I rent the `openai/gpt-6-sol` foundation model through OpenRouter. I do not use RAG because my selected rule set is short enough to pass directly in the prompt, and I do not fine-tune because 60 synthetic cases are not a suitable training corpus. I went directly to code rather than trialling a low-code builder because this experiment needs repeatable dataset validation, hiding gold labels, schema checks, and controlled cost accounting; I did not test a low-code tool. My API estimate is US$0.00792 per case on the new 30-case extension. I did not measure development time-to-deploy or current reviewer labor, so I cannot yet calculate a business ROI.

I implemented a local browser workbench and a single-script command that accept one structured script, make two sequential model calls (evidence extraction, then rule-based decision), and return one structured review. The browser loads a saved example without calling the API; a live review requires an explicit cost confirmation and is blocked above its per-script ceiling. The two-stage separation supports evidence traceability, but it does not meet the Watchouts document's literal one-model-call version of the smallest first slice. It also doubles call count relative to a one-call design. I retain it in this evaluation because the instructor's interim feedback endorsed keeping evidence assertions separate from policy judgement; I treat the extra call and its cost as a design trade-off.

The browser workflow now lets the reviewer record a final disposition and download a JSON audit record. It captures the script, the CHANEL public source and project rule references, available keyword and AI findings, the human decision, and timestamps. The record is created locally in the browser and downloaded on demand; there is no server-side history or team audit log. If the draft changes, the interface clears the old findings and disables export until the new version is reviewed and decided. The interface also states that no campaign brief was provided, so mandatory selling points are not evaluated. This is an explicit scope decision: the public guide cannot supply a private campaign's required product messages, and a public-guideline `PASS` is not campaign approval.

The AI experiment used OpenRouter with `openai/gpt-6-sol`. The revised run processed 30 cases with 36,590 input tokens and 14,058 output tokens. At the recorded rates, estimated usage cost was US$0.213760 total, or about US$0.00713 per case. The provider dashboard should be checked for actual billed cost; an interrupted request may not appear in the saved token totals. This figure excludes human-review labor and production overhead.

The supplementary-extension AI run used the same provider and model and processed 30 cases with 38,229 input tokens and 16,102 output tokens. Estimated usage cost was US$0.237478 total, or about US$0.00792 per case. The evaluator returned valid structured output for all 30 cases. The provider dashboard should be checked for actual billed cost; this estimate excludes human-review labor and production overhead. Saved per-case latency was 9.72 seconds median and 14.58 seconds at the 90th percentile on the primary run; the extension was 10.72 seconds median and 13.94 seconds at the 90th percentile. The workbench displays the extension median, the preflight ceiling, and the saved or live result's own cost and wait; these are observed timings from 30 calls per split under those run conditions, not a production service-level guarantee.

## 4. Evaluation method

The dataset contains 60 synthetic English cases in two balanced splits: the primary 30-case course evaluation and a separately blind-reviewed, frozen 30-case extension. These are constructed examples, not collected creator submissions; the case set is project-authored, and no personal data is included. I did not implement a deterministic case-generation script, so the dataset is not claimed to be reproducible from a generator. The original labels and extension adjudications are preserved with versioned files and manifests. A reviewer supplied the 30 extension labels but did not provide identity or relevant experience; the reported 70% initial agreement is therefore raw agreement from one reviewer return, not verified inter-rater reliability. The extension is supplementary development evidence, not a representative real-world test. `ground_truth` and dataset-split fields are removed from the model payload; automated tests verify both exclusions. I did not run a deliberately leaked-label score because the production path is designed to prevent that leak.

The public guideline page is the source for the rule map; the scripts themselves are synthetic. The prototype does not collect from people or use private campaign materials. Because I did not collect actual creator data, this evaluation does not establish performance on real submissions. A small number of real, permissioned cases would be needed to validate whether the synthetic examples reflect actual reviewer language.

The revised prompt was created after inspecting the initial AI run. Therefore the 90.0% result is development evidence, not an independent estimate of generalization. The cases are synthetic, the sample is small, and several cases probe judgment boundaries. A fresh held-out set is needed for an independent performance claim.

The revised AI did not mark any of the 10 clear violations safe (`FLAG` recall 10/10 on this set), and it did not issue a `FLAG` on any clean case. It sent 2 of 10 clean cases to human review, which is a conservative escalation rather than a false `FLAG`. It escalated 9 of 10 borderline cases. The keyword baseline also flagged all 10 clear violations, but had lower overall accuracy because of errors on other classes.

On the supplementary frozen extension, the keyword baseline scored 20/30 (66.7%): it flagged 9/10 clear violations, incorrectly flagged 2/10 clean cases, and escalated 3/10 borderline cases. The AI reviewer scored 23/30 (76.7%): it flagged all 10 clear violations, marked no clean case `FLAG`, sent 2/10 clean cases and 5/10 borderline cases to human review, and produced valid structured output in all 30 cases. Both systems were correct on 15 cases; AI alone was correct on 8, the baseline alone on 5, and both were wrong on 2. The net difference is three additional correct cases for AI on this set. See [the extension evaluation](holdout_keyword_baseline_results.md) and the raw [AI summary](ai_evaluation_extension_v2_summary.json).

The AI run summary is explicitly marked `DRAFT_DATASET_DEVELOPMENT_RUN`: the prompt was revised after inspecting the primary-set outputs. The extension labels were frozen before its AI run, and the prompt was not changed during that run. This provides a supplementary prompt-locked check on additional fictional cases, but the small synthetic set and project-authored data do not establish real-world performance.

For the AI, `HUMAN_REVIEW` is the abstention outcome: it occurred on 7/30 scripts (23.3%). Of the 10 gold borderline cases, 5 were escalated; 2 clean scripts were also escalated. The other five borderline cases received a definitive verdict, including four `FLAG` outcomes and one `PASS`. These counts show both how often the system abstains and which gold classes it abstains on; they do not reveal the counterfactual answer the model would have given without the abstention. No success threshold was registered before this run. For a future, newly sourced and independently labeled set, I propose setting guardrails in advance: at least 9/10 clear violations flagged, no more than 1/10 clean cases falsely flagged, and at least 7/10 borderline cases routed to human review. These are prospective targets, not targets this run was designed to meet.

On the primary 30-case set, the revised AI was correct where the keyword baseline was wrong on 5 cases; the baseline was correct where the AI was wrong on 2; both were wrong on 1; and both were correct on 22. The net difference is three additional correct cases for the revised AI on that set. The extension comparison is reported separately above. Both results are descriptive; they do not establish that AI is generally superior.

## 6. Error analysis and label uncertainty

The revised AI's primary-set disagreements against the frozen labels were `REVIEW-03`, `REVIEW-08`, and `REVIEW-10`. An earlier targeted reviewer agreed with the frozen `HUMAN_REVIEW` label for `REVIEW-03`, whereas the AI predicted `PASS`; the same reviewer and AI chose `HUMAN_REVIEW` for `REVIEW-08` and `REVIEW-10`, which were frozen as `PASS`. This motivated a broader check of how first-hand use and competitor impartiality were applied.

A later 11-case blind reviewer agreed with 7/11 current overall labels. The subsequent [rule-by-rule adjudication](boundary_case_adjudication_v1.md) made different decisions on four overall labels: `REVIEW-03` (`HUMAN_REVIEW` to `PASS`), `REVIEW-08` and `REVIEW-10` (`PASS` to `HUMAN_REVIEW`), and `HOLDOUT-06` (`HUMAN_REVIEW` to `FLAG` because the proposed video lacks spoken and on-screen sponsorship disclosure). It also identified a missing disclosure-placement rule ID on `HOLDOUT-17`, whose overall `FLAG` remains. This is a post-evaluation analysis of selected cases, not a new independent benchmark.

The original frozen datasets and reported scores have not been changed. The later reviewer did not supply a review date or relevant background, so 7/11 is raw agreement on deliberately difficult cases, not verified inter-rater reliability. A [retrospective sensitivity check](label_adjudication_sensitivity_v1.md) shows that applying the four proposed changes to saved predictions would give AI 30/30 on the primary split, while both splits lose their 10/10/10 class balance. All four changes match the already inspected AI predictions, so this hypothetical score is not independent performance evidence and is excluded from the headline results. Incorporating the adjudicated decisions requires a new dataset version and checksum. Until then, all numbers in Section 5 are explicitly scores against the original frozen labels.

## 7. Limitations and next evaluation

My primary evaluation uses 30 synthetic English cases from a narrow interpretation of one public guide; a separate 30-case extension provides supplementary development evidence. My results do not show performance on real creator submissions, other brands, languages, visual content, or audio. I revised the prompt after seeing initial errors. A later adjudication proposed four overall label changes across the two splits; those decisions are documented separately and are not included in the scores above. The downloadable review record is a local handoff artifact, not tamper-proof persistence or a production audit trail. My estimated token cost is not a substitute for checking the provider invoice. The recorded latency distribution covers only these local API runs and does not establish performance under other loads or network conditions.

| Risk or failure mode | Current mitigation | Residual limitation |
|---|---|---|
| A clear rule violation is silently marked safe | Human-adjudicated cases, per-class confusion matrices, and a `HUMAN_REVIEW` outcome; tests ensure labels are withheld from model input | A small synthetic set cannot establish production miss rates; HOLDOUT-26 shows a borderline case can still be passed |
| A borderline or clean script is unnecessarily flagged or escalated | Keep `FLAG` distinct from `HUMAN_REVIEW`; report clean escalations and borderline routing separately | On this extension, four borderline cases were flagged and two clean cases escalated |
| The script looks compliant but the finished video's disclosure is inaudible or unreadable | State that the tool only reviews structured text; require a human to inspect final video and audio | The prototype cannot evaluate timing, legibility, or audibility |
| Unsupported product claims are treated as proven facts | Ask for evidence and route unsubstantiated claims to human review rather than asserting that they are false | The tool cannot independently verify a claim without authoritative source material |
| Users over-trust a confident model verdict | Show quoted evidence and rule IDs, preserve human approval, and expose false-negative and escalation metrics | There is no production monitoring or real-world reviewer study |

The human-review design is consistent with the human-involvement and operations-management themes in Singapore's [Model AI Governance Framework, second edition](https://www.pdpc.gov.sg/-/media/files/pdpc/pdf-files/resource-for-organisation/ai/sgmodelaigovframework2.pdf). This is a governance reference for the design, not a claim of certification or legal compliance.

The extension evaluation is complete and the prompt should stay locked while these results are reported. A permissioned, independently labeled real-world set is future work, not part of this submission. Check actual API billing if available, and report per-class precision/recall and reviewer workload where the dataset supports them. The interface should continue to show evidence and uncertainty so the human reviewer can challenge the model.
